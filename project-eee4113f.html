Engineering System Design — Southern Ocean Carbon Sensor

A multidisciplinary engineering design project focused on developing a low-cost carbon flux sensor for long-term environmental monitoring in the Southern Ocean.

What was the project?

The 2026 engineering design challenge focused on polar exploration and environmental monitoring. Working in a team of four, we collaborated with Dr Sandy Thomalla from UCT's Oceanography department to develop a device capable of collecting high-resolution, long-term carbon flux data in the Southern Ocean.

The project had to be completed within a R2000 budget and a 12-week development period, while prioritising locally sourced components. These constraints meant that the system had to be carefully designed around cost, power consumption, physical size and reliability.

System design

The overall system was divided into four black-box subsystems: sensing, mechanical, control and post-processing, and power. Each subsystem had its own requirements while needing to integrate with the rest of the system.

The sensing subsystem used light transmission and reception to estimate particulate organic carbon. The mechanical subsystem incorporated the housing, wiper mechanism and motorised shutter, while the control and post-processing subsystem handled switching, temperature sensing and data logging.

My contribution — Power Subsystem

I was responsible for designing the power subsystem. The design was based around a 3S1P rechargeable battery capable of supplying a maximum voltage of 12.6V, with monitoring required for the individual battery cells.

I designed the power architecture to provide regulated 3.3V, 5V, +12V and −12V rails. Each rail had to remain within 5% of its required voltage across the specified operating load ranges.

I also designed a MOSFET-based power switch with a standby current below 20 µA when off while still being able to handle a peak current of 1.5A when switched on.

Design constraints

One of the biggest challenges was fitting the complete power subsystem onto a 65×65 mm veroboard while keeping the design below the R500 budget allocated to the subsystem.

This required careful component selection, power budgeting and consideration of the physical layout. The design had to provide multiple regulated voltage rails while remaining compact enough to integrate with the other subsystems.

Engineering decisions

The design required balancing several competing requirements: voltage regulation, current capability, efficiency, physical size, cost and low standby power consumption.

Rather than treating the power supply as an isolated circuit, I had to consider how the different voltage rails would interact with the requirements of the sensing, mechanical and control subsystems. This helped me understand the importance of power architecture at system level.

What I took from the project

This project showed me what engineering looks like when a design has to work within real constraints. The system had to be affordable, compact, power-efficient and capable of operating as part of a larger environmental monitoring platform.

It also strengthened my ability to think at both subsystem and system level. Designing the power subsystem meant understanding not only my own circuit, but also how the requirements of the other subsystems affected the overall engineering solution.
