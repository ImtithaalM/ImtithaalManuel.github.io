From my very first soldered board in second year to a full group system in fourth year, here's a running log of what I've designed, coded and debugged along the way.

EEE2046F

Embedded Systems — STM32F0 Dev Board

My introduction to embedded systems: I assembled a UCT STM32F0 development board by hand, then wrote the firmware to bring it to life. The board waits with all LEDs off until a start button (SW0) is pressed, then cycles through three display routines — a defined LED pattern sequence, an 8-step scrolling display, and an 8-bit binary counter that increments and wraps back to zero.

Three push buttons (SW1–SW3) select between the display modes, with a checkPB() routine handling debouncing and flag updates, and a central display() function dispatching to the correct routine — including switching cleanly between modes mid-run.

Embedded C Hand soldering GPIO / interrupts
EEE3088F

Design Principles — Power Subsystem PCB

Working in a group, we designed the full power subsystem for a mobile robot (the "MM") in KiCad, with each member owning specific parts of the design for the individual design reports. My module needed to drive four motors bidirectionally — two brushed DC motors at up to 200 mA and two auxiliary motors at up to 500 mA each — while monitoring the battery over I²C with an INA219.

The board also charges a 1S1P battery from a 9V input across two selectable charge rates (≈200 mA and ≈600 mA), takes 9V from a USB-C host input, switches two external 1 A loads, and regulates clean 3.3V and 5V rails at 5% accuracy. A single ON/OFF switch had to cut standby draw below 30 µA while still delivering the robot's 2A peak current on demand.

KiCad Power electronics I²C — INA219 Battery charging

EEE3094S

Control Engineering — The Kitticopter

Yes, really — this lab is themed around Orvillecopter, and the brief is to design a controller that helps a (virtual, thankfully) flying feline track a position input. I started by designing a proportional controller, then evolved it into a lead compensator to meet a tighter set of specifications: steady-state error under 8%, overshoot under 30%, and robustness to ±10% uncertainty in both the system's aerodynamic constant and the physical components used to build the controller — all within a 10V supply limit.

The build shown here is the physical lead compensator circuit, translating the theoretical design into real analogue hardware.

EEE3098S

Engineering Design — Micromouse

A milestone-based robotics build. After assembling the micromouse hardware, Milestone 1 was to design, implement and test a system to move the mouse a specified straight-line distance (2 m) — actuating the motors and using sensor feedback to estimate and correct actual movement — verified in both simulation and a physical hardware demo.

Milestone 2 moved into full maze exploration in simulation: defining requirements, modelling every subsystem, and implementing the logic and control strategy needed for the mouse to completely explore an unknown maze. This project was heavy on MATLAB Simulink and Stateflow work for both the control logic and the simulated environment.

EEE4113F

Engineering System Design — Southern Ocean Carbon Sensor

2026's theme was polar exploration through engineering design. My team of four worked with Dr Sandy Thomalla from UCT's Oceanography department, who needed a device to collect high-resolution, long-term carbon flux data in the Southern Ocean for environmental and oceanographic modelling — built to a R2000 budget and a 12-week timeline, with locally sourced parts.

The system split into four black-box subsystems: sensing (light-signal transmission and reception as a proxy for particulate organic carbon), mechanical (housing, wiper mechanism, motorised shutter), control & post-processing (switching, temperature sensing, data logging), and power — my subsystem. I designed for a 3S1P 12.6V rechargeable battery with per-cell monitoring, regulated 3.3V, 5V, +12V and −12V rails at 5% accuracy across defined load ranges, and a MOSFET-based switch drawing under 20 µA when off while handling 1.5A peak when on — all fit onto a 65×65 mm veroboard for under R500.
